## What it is

A service boundary is the line that says which business rules and which data belong to one service. Drawing it is the first design decision, and the hardest to undo. Domain-driven design (DDD), a way of modelling software around the business, gives two tools for it. A bounded context is an area where one model and its words keep one meaning. An aggregate is a group of objects that is always saved and kept consistent together, such as an order and its order lines.

## Why it exists

If the boundary is wrong, one feature needs changes in several services, which then must be released together, and every order takes many network calls. You pay the network cost from the [previous concept](card:monolith-and-microservices) and get none of the independence. A boundary drawn around one business capability keeps most changes inside one service, so its team can ship alone.

## How it works

| Check | Good sign | Bad sign |
|---|---|---|
| Responsibility | One business capability | A technical layer |
| Calls | Few, coarse calls | Chatty: many calls per operation |
| Release | Deploys alone | Must deploy with others |
| Data | Owns its tables | Reads another service's tables |
| Team | One small team owns it | Many teams edit it |

1. List what the business does: take orders, take payments, keep stock, notify customers. Each of these is a business capability.
2. Find the bounded contexts: look for places where a word such as "Product" changes meaning. One service should not span two contexts.
3. Inside a context, look at the aggregates, such as an Order with its lines. An aggregate is a good first candidate for a service, because a transaction stays inside it.
4. Adjust for other needs, such as team size, scale and security, which may split one service or merge two.
5. Check each candidate against the table above, and redraw the boundary where a bad sign shows up.

## Example

The shop, split by capability, with the word "Product" meaning different things in two contexts:

```text
Order service       orders, order status           -> orders DB
Payment service     payment attempts, refunds      -> payments DB
Inventory service   stock, reservations            -> inventory DB
Notification        emails and SMS to customers    -> no shared tables

"Product" in Catalog   = name, price, photos
"Product" in Inventory = SKU, quantity per warehouse
```

What to notice:

- Each service owns its own data. Order never reads the inventory tables; it asks the Inventory service through its API.
- The two "Product" lines are two models of the same real thing. Each context keeps only the fields it needs, and the product id links them.
- Placing an order touches Order, Inventory and Payment once each. If one order needed ten calls between two services, they would probably belong together.

## Rules and defaults

- **One context per service:** a service's functionality should not span more than one bounded context; if it mixes two models, refine the design.
- **Aggregates and domain services are candidates:** a domain service is a stateless operation across several aggregates, such as a workflow.
- **When unsure, start coarse:** splitting one service into two later is easier than moving functionality across several existing services.
- **No shared tables:** another service's data is reached only through its API.

## Not to be confused with

A business capability is what the business does, such as taking payments. A bounded context is the area where one model and its words mean one thing, such as what "Product" means in Inventory. They often line up, but a capability is about the job and a context is about the language and model. Rule: capability = what the service does; bounded context = where its model keeps one meaning.

## Common mistakes

- **"Smaller services are always better."** Very fine services turn one operation into many network calls. Split only where a boundary removes coordination between teams.
- **"One service per entity: Customer, Address, Order Line."** One business operation then touches all of them, which gives chatty calls and releases in lockstep.

## Interview Qs

### How do you identify service boundaries?

Model the domain with DDD: find the business capabilities and bounded contexts, then the aggregates inside each one. Map each capability to a service that owns its data. Then validate: one responsibility, no chatty calls, it deploys alone, and one small team owns it.

### How small should a microservice be?

Small enough that one small team can build, release and run it alone, and big enough that one business operation does not need chatty calls to other services. Size follows the boundary, not a line count. When unsure, start bigger: splitting one service later is easier than moving code across several.

### What is a bounded context?

A boundary inside which one domain model and its terms stay consistent. The same word, like "Product", can mean different things in two contexts, and each context keeps its own model instead of sharing one big model.
