## What it is

A pattern is a named, proven fix for a problem that keeps coming back. This map lists the problems that appear once one system is split into services, and the pattern that fixes each one, with a link to the concept that teaches it. It is a revision page: read a problem, name the pattern, then say what it costs and what it is often mixed up with.

## Why it exists

Interviewers often describe a problem, such as "the same payment must never be charged twice", and wait for a pattern. Or they name a pattern and ask what it is for. Many names sound alike, such as saga and outbox, or CQRS and event sourcing. Without one map, people mix them up, or use every pattern everywhere and pay for parts they do not need.

## How it works

| A request crosses services | [Distributed tracing](card:distributed-tracing) |

| One instance must coordinate | [Leader election](card:leader-election) |

1. State the problem in one sentence, in business words.
2. Find its row and name the pattern.
3. Say what the pattern costs, such as more moving parts or data that is correct only after a delay.
4. Name the look-alike in brackets and say the difference in one line.

Leader election is the one pattern here without its own concept. Instances of a service compete for a lease, a lock that expires unless it is renewed. The holder does the shared job and keeps renewing; if it dies, the lease runs out and another instance takes over.

## Example

The interviewer says: "Inventory Service sometimes gets the same ReserveStock message twice."

1. Problem: a duplicate delivery, so the stock could be reserved twice for one order.
2. Pattern: an [idempotent consumer](card:delivery-guarantees), often built as an [inbox](card:outbox-and-inbox) table of message ids already processed.
3. Cost: one more table, and a check inside the same transaction as the stock update.
4. Look-alike: the outbox, which sits on the sending side and makes sure the message is sent at all; it cannot stop duplicates.

## Common mistakes

- **Answering with a pattern before stating the problem.** The interviewer cannot tell whether you know why it is needed. Say the problem first.
- **Treating saga and outbox as alternatives.** A saga coordinates a business write across services. An outbox makes one service's save-and-publish reliable. Sagas often use outboxes for their events.

## Interview Qs

### When do you need leader election?

When several instances of a service must agree that only one of them does a shared job, such as a scheduled report, so they do not clash. Use a lease, or a coordination tool such as ZooKeeper. If they only need safe access to one resource, a plain lock is enough.

### What is the difference between CQRS and event sourcing?

CQRS uses separate models for writes and reads, so each can be shaped and scaled for its job. Event sourcing stores every change as an event and rebuilds state from them. They solve different problems: either can be used without the other, and they are often combined.
