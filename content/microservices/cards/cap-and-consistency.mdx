## Why it exists

Data lives as copies on many nodes and in many services. When nodes cannot talk, the system must either wait for the truth or answer anyway.

## How it works

<Mermaid
  alt="Client A writes to node 1 and client B reads from node 2, but the two nodes cannot exchange messages because of a partition. Node 1 must decide whether to accept the write. The CP choice refuses or waits and returns an error or timeout. The AP choice accepts it, and node 2 keeps serving the old value."
  chart={`flowchart LR
  CA([Client A]) --> N1[(Node 1)]
  CB([Client B]) --> N2[(Node 2)]
  N1 -. "partition: no messages" .- N2
  N1 --> Q{Accept the write?}
  Q -- "CP: refuse or wait" --> E([Error or timeout])
  Q -- "AP: accept" --> S([Node 2 serves old data])`}
/>

- Choose per operation, not per system: [strong vs eventual](compare:strong-vs-eventual-consistency).
- A [quorum](card:quorum) lets only the majority side act, which prevents split brain.
- Across services, a [saga](card:saga) gives eventual, not strong, consistency.

## Interview Qs

### Explain CAP with an example.

Two Inventory replicas lose contact. If both keep selling the last unit, they disagree (AP). If one refuses until it reaches the other, it is correct but unavailable (CP).

### How do you keep data consistent across microservices?

Use a local ACID transaction inside each service, then spread the change with events or a saga. Accept eventual consistency where the business allows it, and handle duplicate, late and stale data on purpose ([idempotency](card:idempotency)).

### Does eventual consistency mean the data is wrong?

No. If updates stop and messages get through, all copies reach the same value. Stale reads are a temporary window the design must tolerate.
