## What it is

A metric is a number recorded over time, such as requests per second or how long requests take. An SLI (service level indicator) is a metric chosen because users feel it, such as the share of checkouts that succeed. An SLO (service level objective) is the target for that SLI, and an SLA (service level agreement) is a contract with a customer that has a penalty if missed. The gap between 100% and the SLO is the error budget.

## Why it exists

"Is the shop healthy?" gets a different answer from every team unless there is a number everyone agrees on. Without one, the product team pushes features, the operations team pushes stability, and the argument never ends. An SLO turns user experience into a target, and the error budget settles the argument: while budget remains, ship; once it is spent, fix reliability first.

## How it works

<Compare id="sli-vs-slo-vs-sla" />

1. Pick what users care about in the shop: checkouts that succeed and are fast.
2. Define the SLI: the share of checkout requests that succeed in under 300 ms, measured at the gateway.
3. Set the SLO: 99.9% of them, over a 30-day window.
4. Work out the error budget: 0.1% of requests may fail or be slow in that window.
5. Alert when the budget is being used up fast, for example after a bad release.
6. When the budget is spent, pause feature releases and put the effort into tests, resilience and performance.

## Example

The error budget for the checkout SLO, in numbers.

```text
SLO:          99.9% of checkout requests succeed, 30-day window
Error budget: 0.1% of requests
Traffic:      1,000,000 requests in 30 days  -> 1,000 may fail
As time:      0.1% of 30 days = 43.2 minutes of full outage
```

What to notice:

- The budget is a count of requests: with 1,000,000 checkouts, 1,000 may fail. Double the traffic and the allowed failures double too.
- 43.2 minutes is the same budget spent all at once, as a full outage. Ten small incidents of 5 minutes each would go over it.
- Raise the SLO to 99.99% and the budget falls to about 4.3 minutes a month, so every release becomes far riskier.

## Rules and defaults

- **Golden signals:** latency (track failed requests' latency apart, because fast errors make it look better), traffic, errors (including wrong content and responses too slow for policy) and saturation, how full the most limited resource is.
- **Percentiles:** record latency as histogram buckets. To get a p99 across instances, add the buckets, then compute; never average per-instance percentiles.
- **Safety margin:** keep the internal SLO tighter than anything you promise customers in an SLA, so you can fix problems before the contract is broken.
- **Choosing a target:** do not copy today's performance; start with a few SLOs, and tighten them as you learn.

## Not to be confused with

The four golden signals are a starting set for any user-facing service. RED (rate, errors, duration) is a shorter set for each service's requests. USE (utilisation, saturation, errors) is for each resource, such as a CPU, a disk or a connection pool. Rule: RED for services, USE for machines and pools.

## Common mistakes

- **"The SLO should be 100%."** It is more than users want or notice: on a phone that is itself 99% reliable, a user cannot tell 99.99% from 99.999%. Each extra nine can cost far more than the last and slows releases.
- **"Page someone when CPU passes 80%."** High CPU may harm no user. Page on symptoms such as failing checkouts, and use cause metrics to debug.

## Interview Qs

### What are the four golden signals?

Latency: time per request, with failures tracked apart. Traffic: demand, such as requests per second. Errors: failed, wrong or too-slow responses. Saturation: how full the tightest resource is, such as CPU, memory or a connection pool. If you can measure only four things for a user-facing service, Google SRE says measure these.

### What happens when the error budget runs out?

While budget remains, releases go out as normal. Once it is spent, the error budget policy pauses feature releases, except urgent or security fixes, until the service is back within its SLO. The team spends that time on reliability work. This makes reliability a shared rule instead of a debate.

### How would you choose an SLO for the checkout?

Start from what users feel: checkouts that succeed and respond quickly, measured near the user, such as at the gateway. Do not copy today's numbers. Pick a target the business needs, keep it simple, set a few SLOs only, and adjust as you learn.
