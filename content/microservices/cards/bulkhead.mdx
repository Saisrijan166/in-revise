## What it is

A bulkhead gives each dependency its own limited share of the caller's resources, such as threads, connections or calls running at the same time. In the Order service, calls to Payment may use at most 10 slots and calls to Inventory another 10. When a share is full, extra calls are rejected at once instead of waiting. The name comes from the walls that split a ship's hull into watertight compartments.

## Why it exists

Order handles requests with a fixed pool of threads. If Payment hangs and all outgoing calls share those threads, Payment calls soon hold every one, and even requests that need only Inventory cannot get a thread. A [circuit breaker](card:circuit-breaker) reacts only after enough calls have failed or been slow. A bulkhead limits the damage from the first slow call: Payment can block only its own 10 slots.

## How it works

1. You give each dependency its own limit: 10 concurrent calls for Payment, 10 for Inventory.
2. A call to Payment takes a slot before it starts and gives it back when it ends.
3. Payment becomes slow, so its calls hold their slots longer, and all 10 fill up.
4. The 11th Payment call cannot get a slot. With no waiting allowed, it is rejected at once, and Order returns a fallback or a clear error.
5. Inventory calls still use their own 10 slots, so pages that need only Inventory keep working.

## Example

The Order service's Resilience4j settings in a Spring Boot `application.yml`:

```yaml
resilience4j:
  bulkhead:
    instances:
      payment:
        maxConcurrentCalls: 10   # an 11th call at the same moment is rejected
        maxWaitDuration: 0ms     # do not wait for a free slot
      inventory:
        maxConcurrentCalls: 10   # its own 10 slots, untouched by Payment
```

What to notice:

- `payment` and `inventory` are two separate instances, so each dependency has its own count of calls in flight.
- `maxWaitDuration: 0ms` makes a full bulkhead reject at once. A longer wait would hold the caller's thread, which is the problem the bulkhead exists to prevent.
- These are semaphore bulkheads: the call runs on the caller's own thread, and the bulkhead only counts.

## Rules and defaults

| What | Default | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concurrent calls | 25, no waiting for a slot | Resilience4j 2.x semaphore bulkhead |
| Thread pool | max threads = CPU count, core threads = CPU count − 1, queue of 100 | Resilience4j 2.x thread-pool bulkhead |
| When full | throws `BulkheadFullException` | Resilience4j 2.x |

## Interview Qs

### Semaphore bulkhead or thread-pool bulkhead?

A semaphore bulkhead caps concurrent calls on the caller's own thread and suits any threading model. A thread-pool bulkhead runs calls on its own fixed threads with a bounded queue and returns a `CompletionStage`, so the caller's thread is not held while the call runs.

### Where else can you apply the bulkhead idea?

A separate connection pool for each downstream service, separate instances for important clients, separate queues for each kind of work, and containers with their own CPU and memory limits. Each one stops a single noisy part from using up a shared resource.
