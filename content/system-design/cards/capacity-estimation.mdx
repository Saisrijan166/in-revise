## What it is

Back-of-the-envelope estimation is quick rounded arithmetic to work out the scale of a system before designing it: how many requests per second, how much storage, how much network bandwidth. It is deliberately rough. The aim is not an exact answer but the right order of magnitude, so you can tell which design is needed, for example whether the data fits on one machine or must be split across many.

## Why it exists

A design choice depends on scale, and you build on the ideas from the last two concepts. [Throughput](card:latency-and-throughput) in requests per second decides how many servers you need; total storage decides whether one database is enough or you must [scale](card:scalability) it out. Guessing the architecture without a number risks building for the wrong size. A one-minute estimate with round numbers shows which side of each line you are on, so the rest of the design is grounded.

## How it works

<Steps label="From users to numbers">
<Step title="State assumptions">
Write down daily active users, actions per user, item size and how long data is kept, each with its unit.
</Step>
<Step title="Requests per second">
Requests a day ÷ 86,400 gives the average per second. Multiply by a peak factor, such as 2, for busy periods.
</Step>
<Step title="Storage and bandwidth">
Storage: new items a day × item size × days kept × copies. Bandwidth: requests per second × response size.
</Step>
<Step title="Decide">
Compare each number with what one machine can do, and design for the part that breaks first.
</Step>
</Steps>

1. Start from the assumptions, because every later number depends on them; saying them aloud lets the interviewer correct a wrong one early.
2. Convert daily totals to per-second rates by dividing by 86,400 (round to 100,000 for fast math).
3. Apply the peak factor, because real traffic clusters at busy hours, not a flat average.
4. Work out storage and bandwidth, then decide which component is stressed first.

So the key is to estimate only the numbers that change a decision, and round hard everywhere else.

## Example

A posts app. All sizes are decimal here (1 KB = 1,000 bytes).

```text
Assume:  10 M daily users; each reads 20 posts and writes 1
         1 post = 1 KB; keep posts for 5 years

Writes:   10 M a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 116/s      peak (2x) ≈ 230/s
Reads:   200 M a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 2,300/s    peak (2x) ≈ 4,600/s
Storage:  10 M × 1 KB = 10 GB a day → 3.65 TB a year → about 18 TB in 5 years
Read bandwidth: 2,300/s × 1 KB ≈ 2.3 MB/s ≈ 18 Mbps
```

What to notice:

- Reads outnumber writes 20 to 1, so caching and read replicas pay off first; this ratio often decides the whole read path.
- The per-second rates (a few thousand reads) are modest, so you do not need to shard the database for throughput yet; the storage (18 TB over five years) is the number that will eventually force sharding.
- Bandwidth is quoted in bits (18 Mbps) but computed from bytes (2.3 MB/s): multiply bytes by 8. Mixing the two is the most common estimation slip.

## Rules and defaults

- **Seconds in a day:** 86,400, which you can round to 100,000 (10^5) for mental math.
- **Peak factor:** there is no fixed value; state your own (2x–10x) based on how spiky the traffic is.
- **Powers of two:** 2^10 ≈ a thousand, 2^20 ≈ a million, 2^30 ≈ a billion, 2^40 ≈ a trillion. Use them for storage sizes.
- **Bits vs bytes:** 8 bits = 1 byte, so 1 Gbps = 125 MB/s. Network speeds are in bits, storage in bytes.
- **Round aggressively:** 99,987 ÷ 9.1 becomes 100,000 ÷ 10. Precision is not expected or useful.

## Interview Qs

### How do you estimate requests per second?

Multiply daily active users by requests per user to get requests a day. Divide by 86,400 (or about 100,000 for quick math) to get the average per second. Then multiply by a stated peak factor, such as 2x or 5x, because real traffic is spiky and you must size the system for busy hours, not the quiet average.

### How do you estimate storage?

Multiply new items a day by average item size, then by how many days you keep them, then by the number of copies you store. For example, 10 million 1 KB posts a day is 10 GB a day, about 3.65 TB a year before replication. This tells you whether one database suffices or the data must be sharded.

### Which numbers should you know by heart?

86,400 seconds in a day. Powers of two: 2^10 ≈ a thousand up to 2^40 ≈ a trillion. 8 bits in a byte, so 1 Gbps = 125 MB/s. Rough latencies: a memory read about 100 ns, a datacentre round trip about 0.5 ms, and California to the Netherlands and back about 150 ms.

### When should you actually do the math in an interview?

When a number changes the design, such as whether the data fits on one machine or must be sharded, or whether the read rate needs a cache. Round hard, say your assumptions aloud, get the order of magnitude, and move on. Interviewers want sound reasoning about scale, not precise figures.
