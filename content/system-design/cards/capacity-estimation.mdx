## Why it exists

Design choices depend on scale: one database or many, a cache or not. A one-minute estimate with round numbers shows which side of the line you are on.

## How it works

<Steps label="From users to numbers">
<Step title="State assumptions">
Daily active users, actions per user, item size, how long data is kept. Write them down with units.
</Step>
<Step title="Requests per second">
Requests a day ÷ 86,400. Multiply by a peak factor, such as 2.
</Step>
<Step title="Storage and bandwidth">
Storage: new items a day × size × days kept. Bandwidth: requests per second × response size.
</Step>
<Step title="Decide">
Compare the numbers with what one machine can do, and design for the part that breaks first.
</Step>
</Steps>

- **Round freely:** 99,987 ÷ 9.1 becomes 100,000 ÷ 10. Precision is not expected.

## Example

A posts app. All sizes are decimal (1 KB = 1,000 bytes).

```text
Assume:  10 M daily users; each reads 20 posts and writes 1
         1 post = 1 KB; keep posts for 5 years

Writes:   10 M a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 116/s     peak (2x) ≈ 230/s
Reads:   200 M a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 2,300/s   peak (2x) ≈ 4,600/s
Storage:  10 M × 1 KB = 10 GB a day → 3.65 TB a year → about 18 TB in 5 years
Read bandwidth: 2,300/s × 1 KB ≈ 2.3 MB/s ≈ 18 Mbps
```

Reads outnumber writes 20 to 1, so caching and read replicas pay off first.

## Interview Qs

### How do you estimate QPS?

Daily active users × requests per user gives requests a day. Divide by 86,400, or about 100,000 for quick math, to get the average per second. Then multiply by a peak factor you state, such as 2.

### How do you estimate storage?

New items a day × average size × days kept, then multiply by the number of copies you keep. For example, 10 million 1 KB posts a day is 10 GB a day, about 3.65 TB a year before copies.

### Which numbers should you know by heart?

86,400 seconds a day. Powers of two: 2^10 is about a thousand, up to 2^40, about a trillion. 8 bits a byte, so 1 Gbps is 125 MB/s. Rough latencies: memory 100 ns, datacentre round trip 0.5 ms, California to the Netherlands and back 150 ms.

### When should you do the math in an interview?

When a number changes the design, such as whether data fits on one machine or must be sharded. Round hard, say your assumptions, and move on. Interviewers do not expect precision.
