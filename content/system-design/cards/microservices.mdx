## Why it exists

As one codebase grows, a small change needs a full rebuild and redeploy, teams block each other, and you must scale the whole app to scale one busy part.

## How it works

<Compare id="monolith-vs-microservices" />

- **Split by business capability:** orders, payments, search, each owned by one team. Conway's law: systems end up shaped like the teams that build them.
- **Database per service:** a service's tables are private; others use its API or events. A change across services becomes a [saga](card:saga), not one ACID transaction.
- **Modular monolith:** one deploy, strict boundaries between modules. Shopify chose this to get modularity without more deployment units.
- **Strangler fig:** a facade routes one feature at a time to new services until the old app can retire.

## Example

A shop has orders, payments and search; today one copy of each part is enough. On sale day, search traffic grows 10 times.

- **Monolith:** run 10 copies of the whole app, payments code included.
- **Microservices:** run 10 search instances and keep one of everything else. But checkout now calls payments over the network, so it needs timeouts, retries and tracing.

## Interview Qs

### When would you choose microservices over a monolith?

When many teams must ship independently, parts have very different scaling needs, and domain boundaries are clear. For a new product or a small team, a well-modularised monolith is faster; split it later.

### How do microservices share data?

They do not share tables. Each service owns its data and exposes it through its API or events. Cross-service reads call each API and join in code (API composition) or use a CQRS view; cross-service writes use sagas.

### What are the main costs of microservices?

Network latency and partial failure, no ACID across services, harder testing and debugging, and heavy operations: CI/CD, orchestration, monitoring and distributed tracing. Fowler calls this the microservice premium.

### How do you move a monolith to microservices?

Use the strangler fig pattern. Put a facade in front, move one capability at a time to a new service, route its traffic there, then remove the old code. Clients keep using the same interface throughout.
