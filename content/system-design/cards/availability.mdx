## What it is

Availability is how reliable a service is, written as the share of time it is up, or the share of requests that succeed. It is usually given as "nines": 99.9% is three nines, 99.99% is four nines. Three related terms go with it. An SLI is the measured number (successful requests ÷ all requests). An SLO is the target you set for that number. An SLA is the contract you promise customers, with a penalty if you miss it.

## Why it exists

Nothing is up 100% of the time, so "reliable" needs an exact number the team agrees on. Without one, nobody can say whether the payment service is reliable enough, or decide when to stop adding features and fix reliability instead. Availability targets give that number. The [error budget](term:error-budget), the small amount of failure the target allows, becomes the rule for that decision: while budget is left, you ship; once it is spent, you fix.

## How it works

| Availability | Downtime a year | Downtime a month (30 days) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | 3.65 days | 7.2 hours |
| 99.9% | 8.76 hours | 43.2 minutes |
| 99.95% | 4.38 hours | 21.6 minutes |
| 99.99% | 52.6 minutes | 4.32 minutes |
| 99.999% | 5.26 minutes | 25.9 seconds |

1. Choose how to measure it: by time (up time ÷ total time) or by requests (good ÷ total). Google prefers request success rate, because a big service is rarely fully down but can fail a fraction of requests.
2. Set the SLO (the target) on that SLI, over a window such as four weeks.
3. The error budget is 1 − SLO: the share of failures allowed in the window.
4. For parts in series, where every part must work, multiply their availabilities (they drop). For parts in parallel, where one copy is enough, multiply their failure rates (availability rises).

So the key is that a chain is only as available as the product of its links, which is why redundancy matters.

## Example

Three ways the numbers play out for the payment service.

```text
Error budget:  SLO = 99.9% of requests succeed over 4 weeks.
               3 million requests → 3000 may fail.
               A bad deploy fails 2000 → two-thirds of the budget gone.

Faster recovery:  MTBF 150 days (3600 h), MTTR 1 h → 3600 / 3601 ≈ 99.97%.
                  Cut MTTR to 30 min → about 99.986%. Recovering faster lifts availability.

A real SLA:    Amazon EC2 promises 99.99% monthly uptime per Region
               (instances in two or more zones). Below that, customers
               get 10%, 30% or 100% back as service credits.
```

What to notice:

- The error budget turns a percentage into a concrete count (3000 failures), so a team can see how much one bad deploy costs.
- Availability rises either by failing less often (higher MTBF) or by recovering faster (lower [MTTR](term:mttr)); recovery speed is often the easier lever.
- The SLA (99.99%, with credits) is the external promise; teams set a tighter internal SLO so they react before the SLA is breached.

## Rules and defaults

- **Nines rule:** each extra nine allows 10 times less downtime and costs much more to reach. 99.99% is about 52.6 minutes of downtime a year.
- **Series (hard dependencies):** multiply availabilities. 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ 99.8%, lower than either part.
- **Parallel (redundancy):** multiply failure rates. Two independent 99.9% copies give 1 − 0.001 × 0.001 = 99.9999%, but only if they fail independently (different zones).
- **SLA looser than SLO:** set the internal SLO tighter than the customer SLA, so you fix problems before the contract is broken.

## Not to be confused with

This availability (an uptime percentage) is not the "A" in the [CAP theorem](card:cap-theorem). Here, availability is measured over time: the share of requests that succeed. CAP availability is a strict yes-or-no property during a network partition: every live node must answer every request, even with stale data. A database can be highly available in the percentage sense yet count as "CP" in CAP. Rule: this page's availability is a percentage; CAP's is a guarantee about behaviour during a partition.

## Interview Qs

### What does "four nines" mean?

99.99% available: about 52.6 minutes of downtime a year, or 4.32 minutes a month on a 30-day month. Each extra nine allows 10 times less downtime than the one before, and costs far more in redundancy and engineering to reach, so you pick the fewest nines the business actually needs.

### SLA vs SLO vs SLI?

The SLI is the measurement, such as the share of successful requests. The SLO is the internal target for that measurement, such as 99.9% over four weeks. The SLA is the contract with customers, with penalties such as service credits, and it is usually looser than the SLO so the team has room to react before breaching it.

### Two 99.9% services: chained, and redundant?

Chained, where both must work, multiply availabilities: 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ 99.8%, lower than either. Redundant, where one is enough, multiply failure rates: 1 − 0.001 × 0.001 = 99.9999%. The redundant result only holds if they fail independently, for example in different availability zones, not the same rack.

### What is an error budget and how is it used?

It is 1 minus the SLO: at 99.9%, 0.1% of requests may fail in the window. While budget remains, the team keeps shipping changes. Once it is spent, releases pause and reliability work takes priority, except for urgent and security fixes. It turns reliability into a shared, measurable decision rule.
