## What it is

Disaster recovery (DR) is the plan and the infrastructure for bringing a whole workload back after a rare, large failure: an entire region going down, a bad deploy that deletes data, or ransomware. It is driven by two agreed numbers. The [RPO](term:rpo) is how much data you may lose, measured as a length of time. The [RTO](term:rto) is how long you may be down before the service is back. Everything else follows from those two targets.

## Why it exists

[Redundancy](card:redundancy-failover) within one region survives a failed server or zone, but not the loss of the whole region. And replicas are no help against a bad delete or corruption, because they copy the damage instantly. Without a DR plan, either event can lose your data permanently or keep you down for days. DR sets an agreed limit on both the data loss and the downtime, and builds the backups or standby site needed to stay inside it.

## How it works

<Compare id="rpo-vs-rto" />

| Strategy (AWS) | The recovery site has | RPO | RTO |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Backup and restore | Backups only; redeploy everything | Hours | 24 hours or less |
| Pilot light | A live data copy; app servers switched off | Minutes | Tens of minutes |
| Warm standby | A small but working copy, always running | Seconds | Minutes |
| Multi-site active-active | Full copies, all serving traffic | Near zero | Potentially zero |

1. Agree the RPO and RTO for each workload with the business.
2. Pick the highest row in the table (cheapest) whose RPO and RTO still meet those targets.
3. Keep point-in-time backups in a separate region and account, so they survive a region loss or stolen credentials.
4. Rebuild the recovery site from infrastructure as code, so recovery does not depend on remembered manual steps.

So the key is to buy only the strictness you need: cost and complexity climb steeply down the table.

## Example

Orders database, with the business targets RPO 15 minutes, RTO 1 hour:

- **Nightly backup at 02:00 only:** a failure at 23:00 loses about 21 hours of orders, which fails the 15-minute RPO.
- **Amazon RDS automated backups:** transaction logs reach storage every 5 minutes, so a point-in-time restore loses minutes, not hours, and meets the RPO.

What to notice:

- The restore creates a brand-new DB instance, so you must time that restore in a real drill to prove it fits inside the 1-hour RTO; the RTO is not met just because backups exist.
- For a whole-region outage, copy the backups to a second region, or keep a pilot light running there, so the data and a place to restore it both survive.

## Rules and defaults

| What | Default | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Automated backup retention | 7 days (console) or 1 day (API/CLI); 0-35 allowed, 0 turns backups off | Amazon RDS |
| Transaction logs copied to storage | Every 5 minutes | Amazon RDS |

- **High availability is not disaster recovery:** HA keeps you up through everyday faults (a failed node or zone) using redundancy and failover; DR recovers from rare disasters and from data corruption. You need both.

## Not to be confused with

High availability and disaster recovery both improve reliability, but HA handles frequent, small faults automatically with redundancy, while DR handles rare, large disasters, often with human steps. Replication copies live changes between nodes; a backup is a point-in-time snapshot you can roll back to. Rule: HA for the common case, DR for the catastrophe, backups for the mistakes replication would copy.

## Common mistakes

- **"Replicas in two regions mean we are safe."** Replication copies every change, including a bad delete or a corrupting bug, to every region at once. Only point-in-time backups, ideally in another account and region, let you return to before the damage.
- **"Backups exist, so we are covered."** A backup you have never restored may be incomplete, unreadable, or slower to restore than your RTO allows. Restore on a schedule, check the data, and time it against the RTO.

## Interview Qs

### RPO versus RTO: what do they mean?

RPO is how much data you can afford to lose, measured as the time back to the last good copy; hourly backups give an RPO of up to one hour. RTO is how long you can be down until service is restored. RPO is set by how often you back up or replicate; RTO is set by how fast you detect, fail over or restore.

### Which disaster recovery strategy would you choose?

The cheapest one that still meets the agreed RPO and RTO: backup-and-restore when hours are acceptable, pilot light or warm standby when you need minutes, multi-site active-active for near-zero loss and downtime. Going stricter than the business needs only adds cost and operational complexity for no benefit.

### We have replicas in two regions. Do we still need backups?

Yes. Replication copies every change, including a bad delete, a corrupting bug, or ransomware encryption, to all regions instantly. Point-in-time backups, ideally held in a separate account and region, are the only way to return to a moment before the damage. HA and replication protect against infrastructure failure, not against mistakes.
