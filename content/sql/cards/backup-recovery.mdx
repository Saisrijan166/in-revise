## Why it exists

Disks die, servers crash, and people run a bad DELETE. High availability handles the crash; only a backup undoes the mistake, because a [replica](card:replication) copies it at once.

## How it works

<Steps label="Point-in-time recovery after a DROP TABLE at 11:00">
  <Step title="Base backup">Take a physical copy regularly, such as with pg_basebackup.</Step>
  <Step title="Keep the log">Archive every WAL file (PostgreSQL) or keep the binary logs (MySQL).</Step>
  <Step title="Restore">Put the latest base backup from before 11:00 on a server.</Step>
  <Step title="Replay">Apply the log up to 10:59, just before the mistake.</Step>
</Steps>

- The recovery stop point must be after the base backup finished.

## Example

```text
# PostgreSQL 18: in postgresql.conf, plus a recovery.signal file
restore_command = 'cp /mnt/archive/%f %p'
recovery_target_time = '2026-10-20 10:59:00+05:30'
# MySQL: restore the full backup, then
#   mysqlbinlog --stop-datetime="2026-10-20 10:59:00" \
#     <binlogs written after the backup> | mysql
```

| What | Default | Where |

## Interview Qs

### What is the difference between RPO and RTO?

RPO is how much data, measured in time, you can afford to lose: hourly backups mean up to an hour. RTO is how long the service may stay down before it is back.

### How do you recover a table someone dropped an hour ago?

Restore the last base backup on a separate server and replay the WAL or binlog to just before the DROP. Then copy the table back, or switch over to the restored server.
