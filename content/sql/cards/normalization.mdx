## Why it exists

When one fact is copied into many rows, a change must reach every copy. Miss one and the data contradicts itself.

## How it works

<Steps label="Normalise one orders table">
  <Step title="Start">orders(order_id, user_id, user_email, products = 'pen, book'): a list in one cell.</Step>
  <Step title="1NF">One row per product: order_lines(order_id, product_id, product_name, qty, user_id, user_email), key (order_id, product_id).</Step>
  <Step title="2NF">product_name depends on product_id alone, the user on order_id alone. Move them out: products, orders; order_lines keeps qty.</Step>
  <Step title="3NF">In orders, user_email depends on user_id, not on the order. Move it to users(id, email).</Step>
  <Step title="Result">users, orders, products, order_lines: each fact once, joined by [foreign keys](card:keys).</Step>
</Steps>

- Each step removes one kind of dependency: partial (2NF), then transitive (3NF).
- 2NF only matters when the key has more than one column.

## Example

The update anomaly in the unnormalised table:

```text
order_id | user_id | user_email
1        | 7       | asha@new.in
2        | 7       | asha@old.in   -- one copy was missed
```

## Interview Qs

### What problems does normalisation solve?

Three anomalies. Update: a fact stored many times must change everywhere. Insert: you cannot store a user until they order. Delete: removing the last order also loses the user.

### Explain 1NF, 2NF and 3NF.

1NF: each cell holds one value. 2NF: no column depends on part of a composite key. 3NF: no non-key column depends on another non-key column.

### How is BCNF different from 3NF?

BCNF requires the left side of every dependency to be a key. 3NF allows one exception: the dependent column is itself part of a candidate key. So BCNF is slightly stricter.
