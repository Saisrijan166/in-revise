## Why it exists

People and configs use names, but IP needs addresses. A name also lets a service move to new IPs without clients changing.

## How it works

<Mermaid
  alt="The app asks the recursive resolver. Root refers it to the .example TLD, which refers it to the shop.example authoritative server, which answers 203.0.113.10, TTL 300."
  chart={`sequenceDiagram
  participant C as App and OS cache
  participant R as Recursive resolver
  participant Root as Root server
  participant T as .example TLD
  participant A as shop.example authoritative
  C->>R: api.shop.example A?
  R->>Root: api.shop.example?
  Root-->>R: ask the .example servers
  R->>T: api.shop.example?
  T-->>R: ask the shop.example servers
  R->>A: api.shop.example A?
  A-->>R: 203.0.113.10, TTL 300
  R-->>C: 203.0.113.10 (cached)`}
/>

- Each hop checks its cache first; most lookups never reach the root.
- Record types: see the [record list](card:dns-records).

## Example

```bash
dig +noall +answer api.shop.example A    # the answer and its remaining TTL
dig +trace api.shop.example              # walk root, TLD, authoritative yourself
```

## Interview Qs

### You changed a DNS record but some users still reach the old IP. Why?

Resolvers keep the cached old answer until its TTL runs out. "Propagation" is just caches expiring; lower the TTL well before a planned move.

### Why can a CNAME not sit at the zone apex?

A CNAME name may hold no other records, but the apex (shop.example) must hold SOA and NS records. Use A or AAAA there.

### When does DNS use TCP?

For answers too big for UDP (the truncated flag makes the client retry), for zone transfers, or when a client simply prefers it. Every DNS implementation must support TCP (RFC 7766).
