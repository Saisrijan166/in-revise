## Why it exists

Before data flows, both ends must know the other is there and agree where byte numbering starts, so they can spot lost, repeated or stale segments.

## How it works

- **TIME_WAIT** re-ACKs a repeated FIN and lets late segments from the old connection die out.
- **RST** aborts a connection at once; it also answers a SYN to a port nobody listens on.
- **SYN flood:** fake SYNs fill the queue of half-open connections; Linux then falls back to SYN cookies.

## Defaults

| What | Default | Where |
|---|---|---|
| TIME_WAIT length | 60 s (constant TCP_TIMEWAIT_LEN, not a sysctl) | Linux |
| SYN cookies | 1: sent only when the SYN queue overflows | Linux `net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies` |

## Interview Qs

### Why three steps and not two?

Each side must send its ISN and see it acknowledged. The server's SYN and ACK share one segment, so it takes three. With two, the server cannot tell a fresh SYN from an old duplicate.

### A client shows thousands of TIME_WAIT sockets. Is that a bug?

No, TIME_WAIT is normal on the side that closes first. Thousands mean a new connection per request, which can use up [ephemeral ports](card:ports-and-sockets). Reuse connections with keep-alive or a [pool](card:connection-pooling).

### What are SYN cookies?

The server stops storing half-open connections. It packs their state into the SYN-ACK's sequence number and rebuilds it from the client's final ACK.
