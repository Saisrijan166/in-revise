## Why it exists

A fast sender can overflow the receiver's buffer, or the routers in between. These are two different limits, so TCP keeps two windows.

## How it works

<Compare id="flow-vs-congestion-control" />

- **Slow start:** cwnd grows by about one segment per ACK, so it roughly doubles each round trip, up to ssthresh.
- **Congestion avoidance:** then about one more segment per round trip; on loss, cut cwnd (AIMD).
- Reno halves cwnd on loss; CUBIC, the usual Linux default, cuts it to 0.7 and regrows along a cubic curve.

## Example

A Reno-style sawtooth, with ssthresh at 80 segments:

```text
round trip:  1   2   3   4   5   6   7   8   9
cwnd:       10  20  40  80  81  82  83  41  42
            slow start ---| +1 per trip -| loss: halve, then +1 again
```

## Defaults

| What | Default | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Initial cwnd | min(10 × MSS, max(2 × MSS, 14,600 bytes)) | RFC 6928 |
| Algorithm | set by kernel config; cubic on mainstream distros | Linux `net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control` |
| Slow start after idle | 1: cwnd is reset after an idle period | Linux `net.ipv4.tcp_slow_start_after_idle` |

## Interview Qs

### What is the difference between rwnd and cwnd?

The receiver sets rwnd: how much free buffer it has. The sender sets cwnd: how much it thinks the network can carry. The sender keeps at most the smaller amount unacknowledged.

### Why is a brand-new connection slow, and what do backends do about it?

Slow start begins with a small cwnd and grows each round trip. Reuse warm connections ([pools, keep-alive](card:connection-pooling)); long links need large windows, see [bandwidth-delay product](card:latency-and-bandwidth).
