## Why it exists

One request can match many policies: on the role, the bucket and the organisation. AWS needs one fixed rule to reach a single answer.

## How it works

<Steps label="How AWS decides one request">
  <Step title="Authenticate">AWS checks who signed the request: the principal.</Step>
  <Step title="Look for Deny">An explicit Deny in any policy that applies ends it: denied.</Step>
  <Step title="Guardrails">If the account uses SCPs or RCPs, they must allow the action.</Step>
  <Step title="Find an Allow">An identity-based or resource-based policy must allow it. None? Implicit deny.</Step>
  <Step title="Limits">A permissions boundary or session policy, if present, must allow it too.</Step>
</Steps>

<Compare id="identity-vs-resource-based-policy" />

- Role trust policies and KMS key policies are exceptions: they must allow the principal themselves.
- IAM Access Analyzer validates policies and flags public or cross-account access.

## Interview Qs

### What is the difference between authentication and authorisation in AWS?

Authentication proves who the principal is, from the signed credentials. Authorisation then evaluates the policies to decide whether that principal may perform this action on this resource.

### A role's policy allows s3:GetObject, but the call is denied. What do you check?

An explicit deny in the bucket policy, an SCP or RCP, a boundary or a session policy. Cross-account, the bucket policy must allow the role too. For SSE-KMS objects, the [KMS key](card:kms-and-encryption) must allow decrypt.

### Why does an SCP not grant any permission?

An SCP only caps what principals in member accounts can do. Access still needs an identity-based or resource-based policy that allows it.
