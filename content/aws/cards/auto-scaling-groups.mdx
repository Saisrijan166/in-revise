## What it is

An Auto Scaling group is a managed set of [EC2](card:ec2) instances that AWS keeps at a size you choose. You give it a launch template (the recipe for each instance: AMI, instance type, security groups, user data) and three numbers: minimum, desired and maximum. The group launches instances until it has the desired count, and from then on it watches their health and the load, launching or terminating instances to match.

## Why it exists

A fixed set of servers is wrong most of the time: too few at the daily peak, so requests queue, and too many at 3 a.m., so you pay for idle machines. Worse, if one crashes it stays down until someone notices. An Auto Scaling group fixes both. It replaces a failed instance on its own, so the fleet heals, and it changes the instance count as traffic rises and falls, so capacity follows demand instead of a guess.

1. You create the group with a launch template, a min, desired and max, and subnets in two or more AZs.
2. The group launches instances up to the desired count, spreading them evenly across the AZs.
3. It keeps checking each instance. An instance that fails its health check is terminated, and a replacement is launched, so the count returns to desired.
4. A target tracking policy watches a CloudWatch metric, such as average CPU. When the metric drifts from its target, the policy raises or lowers desired.
5. Changing desired launches new instances or terminates extra ones, always staying between min and max.

So min and max are the safety rails, and desired is the number the group works to keep.

## Example

One target tracking policy keeps the payment-service group near 50% CPU:

```bash
aws autoscaling put-scaling-policy \
  --auto-scaling-group-name payment-asg \
  --policy-name cpu-50 --policy-type TargetTrackingScaling \
  --target-tracking-configuration \
  '{"PredefinedMetricSpecification":
      {"PredefinedMetricType":"ASGAverageCPUUtilization"},
    "TargetValue":50.0}'
```

What to notice:

- You give a target, not step rules. Auto Scaling creates and manages the CloudWatch alarms and works out how many instances to add or remove, like a thermostat holding a temperature.
- `ASGAverageCPUUtilization` is average CPU across the group. For a web tier, `ALBRequestCountPerTarget` (requests per instance) often tracks load better.
- It scales out quickly when CPU is above 50% but scales in gradually, to protect availability. Max capacity still caps how far out it can go.

## Rules and defaults

- **Health check type:** EC2 status checks only, by default; ELB (and VPC Lattice, EBS) checks are opt-in (EC2 Auto Scaling).
- **Replacement:** an instance not in the `running` state is replaced at once; one that fails other checks is replaced a few at a time to avoid replacing the whole group.
- **Across AZs:** the group balances instances evenly across the AZs of the subnets you give it.
- **Scaling policy types:** target tracking (AWS's first choice), step, simple (AWS advises against), scheduled, and predictive; see [the scaling policies in the tree](card:asg-scaling-policies).
- **Not for containers:** ECS services and DynamoDB scale through Application Auto Scaling, not an EC2 Auto Scaling group.

## Not to be confused with

A launch template describes one instance; the Auto Scaling group decides how many of them run and keeps them healthy. A target tracking policy is just the rule that moves the group's desired count. Rule: template = what each instance is; group = how many and how healthy; policy = when the number changes.

## Common mistakes

- **"Scaling and load balancing are the same thing."** A [load balancer](card:elastic-load-balancing) spreads requests over the instances you have; the Auto Scaling group changes how many instances there are. You usually use both: the ALB registers and deregisters instances as the group launches and terminates them.

## Interview Qs

### Traffic suddenly doubles. How does your design cope?

The load balancer spreads requests over the healthy instances, and target tracking adds instances when CPU or requests per target rise above the target, up to max. For a known daily peak, scheduled or predictive scaling adds capacity before it arrives, so the first users of the spike are not slow.

### Why turn on ELB health checks when EC2 checks already exist?

EC2 status checks only confirm the virtual machine is running and not impaired; they cannot see a deadlocked or crashed app. ELB health checks call your app's health path, so a stuck app fails them and the group replaces that instance. Without them a hung app keeps receiving traffic.

### Target tracking or step scaling?

Target tracking: you give a target value and Auto Scaling creates the alarms and adjusts capacity to hold it, like a thermostat. Step scaling: you define the alarms and exactly how much to add per breach size. AWS recommends target tracking for metrics that move with capacity, such as CPU or requests per target, and step scaling when you need that finer control.
