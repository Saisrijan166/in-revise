## What it is

The shared responsibility model is AWS's statement of who secures what. AWS secures the cloud itself: the data centres, the hardware, the software that runs its services, and the network between them. You secure what you run in the cloud: your data, the access rules that decide who can touch it, your encryption choices, your firewall settings and your own code. The exact line depends on the service: the more managed it is, the less is left to you.

## Why it exists

In the cloud, AWS runs part of the stack and you run the rest. Without a clear line, each side can assume the other locked something down, and a gap is left. AWS says plainly "we do this, you do that". AWS patches its hardware, guards its buildings and keeps its network safe; you must still set who can read the payment data, turn on encryption and close the firewall. If you skip your half, your data is exposed even though AWS did everything on its side.

## How it works

The table shows how the split shifts across three services. The more AWS manages, the fewer rows are yours.

| Who does it | EC2 (IaaS) | RDS (managed database) | S3, DynamoDB, Lambda |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buildings, hardware, host | AWS | AWS | AWS |
| Guest OS patches | You | AWS | AWS |
| Database engine or runtime | You install and patch | AWS patches; you pick version and window | AWS |
| Firewall and access rules | You: security groups | You: security groups, IAM | You: IAM, resource policies |
| Your data, who can read it, encryption | You | You | You |

1. Pick what runs your app. On EC2 you get a bare virtual server, so you patch the OS and everything on it.
2. Move to a managed service, such as RDS, and AWS takes over the OS and engine patches; you keep the data and access rules.
3. Move further, to S3 or Lambda, and AWS runs the OS and platform; your job narrows to data, access and encryption, but never disappears.

So the key is: the bottom of the stack is always AWS's, the data and access are always yours, and the middle moves with how managed the service is.

## Rules and defaults

- **Access is never open-by-default on your behalf:** you must set IAM and resource policies. AWS does not guess who should read your data. Your tools are [IAM](card:iam-basics), [KMS encryption](card:kms-and-encryption) and [security groups](card:security-groups-and-nacls).
- **EC2 OS patching is yours:** AWS patches the host it runs on, not the guest OS inside your instance.
- **RDS required patches:** AWS applies security and reliability patches in the weekly maintenance window you set; it does not touch the OS whenever it likes.
- **Lambda managed runtimes:** updated automatically by default (Auto mode). A function packaged as a container image is yours to rebuild from a new base image.

## Interview Qs

### Explain the shared responsibility model.

AWS is responsible for security of the cloud: the buildings, hardware, software and network that run its services. The customer is responsible for security in the cloud: their data, IAM access, encryption settings, firewall rules and, on EC2, the guest OS and installed apps. The line moves by service; the more managed it is, the less the customer does.

### Who patches what on EC2, RDS and Lambda?

On EC2 you patch the guest OS and your software; AWS only patches the host beneath it. On RDS, AWS supplies OS and engine updates and applies required ones in your maintenance window. On Lambda, AWS updates managed runtimes automatically by default; a container-image function you rebuild yourself.

### Using an AWS service that is "PCI compliant" makes our app compliant too, right?

No. AWS being compliant covers its half, the infrastructure. Your application's compliance is still yours: how you store card data, who can access it, encryption and logging. Compliance, like security, is shared, so you inherit AWS's controls but must meet your own.
