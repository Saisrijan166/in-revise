## What it is

AWS pricing models are the different ways you pay for the same compute. On-Demand means you pay by the second while a server runs, with no commitment. A Savings Plan is a discount you unlock by committing to a steady spend, in US dollars per hour, for one or three years. Spot means you run on EC2's spare capacity at a deep discount, but AWS may take it back when it needs it. The instances are identical; only how you pay and the risk differ.

## Why it exists

Your load has parts with different shapes. Some runs all year, some spikes for a sale, some is optional background work. One flat price for all three would be wasteful: you would overpay for the steady base, which is predictable, and you would not benefit from the fact that some work can tolerate interruption. AWS trades discounts for one of two things: a commitment (Savings Plans) or the right to interrupt you (Spot). You match each part of the load to the model that fits it, and pay less overall.

## How it works

<Compare id="on-demand-vs-savings-plans-vs-spot" />

1. Find the steady base: the instances that run all the time. Cover them with a Savings Plan, committing to their hourly spend for a discount.
2. Leave spiky or short-lived load on On-Demand, so you pay only while it runs and stop the moment it ends.
3. Put interruptible work, such as batch jobs, on Spot for the deepest discount, and make it retry if an instance is reclaimed.
4. Watch the extras that do not show up as instance hours: data leaving AWS to the internet, and traffic crossing AZs, which is billed in both directions.

So the key is: commit to what is steady, stay flexible for what is spiky, and use Spot only for what can be interrupted.

## Example

Here is how the payment service's parts map to the three models, as of 23-10-2026.

```text
Payment service workload        Pricing model
--------------------------------------------------------
4 instances, 24x7               Compute Savings Plan   (also covers Fargate, Lambda)
+6 instances on sale days       On-Demand              (Auto Scaling adds and removes them)
nightly receipt rebuild (batch) Spot                   (reclaimed job simply retries)
```

What to notice:

- The steady 4 instances get the commitment discount, because you know they always run.
- The 6 sale-day instances stay On-Demand: committing for a year to capacity you need two days a year would waste money.
- The batch job is the one part safe on Spot: losing an instance costs a retry, not a failed payment.
- A Compute Savings Plan is not tied to these exact instances; it keeps discounting even if you move the work to Fargate or Lambda.

## Rules and defaults

- **On-Demand billing:** per second, with a 60-second minimum, for most Linux and Windows instances.
- **Savings Plans discount:** up to 72% off On-Demand for EC2 Instance Savings Plans (one family, one Region) and up to 66% for Compute Savings Plans (any family, Region, Fargate, Lambda), for a 1- or 3-year term. A Savings Plan is a billing discount; AWS cannot stop your instances because of it.
- **Spot:** up to 90% off; EC2 issues a two-minute interruption notice through EventBridge and the instance metadata before it stops or terminates a Spot Instance.
- **Reserved Instances:** a 1- or 3-year commitment to a specific instance configuration. Not a physical instance, but a billing discount on matching usage. AWS recommends Savings Plans over them for their flexibility.
- **Data transfer:** data out to the internet is charged (the first 100 GB a month is free, across services and Regions); data into AWS is free; traffic between AZs in the same Region is charged on both the sending and receiving side.

## Interview Qs

### What is the difference between Savings Plans and Reserved Instances?

Both are 1- or 3-year commitments with similar discounts, up to 72% off. A Reserved Instance discounts one instance configuration you name. A Savings Plan commits you to a fixed US-dollar-per-hour spend, and a Compute Savings Plan applies across instance family, size, Region, Fargate and Lambda. AWS recommends Savings Plans because they are more flexible.

### When is Spot a bad choice?

For anything that cannot stop at two minutes' notice: a single stateful server, a database, or a long job with no checkpoints. The interruption would cut it off mid-work. Spot suits batch jobs, data processing and background workers that retry, or extra capacity added beside an On-Demand or Savings Plan base.

### The AWS bill jumped this month. Where do you look first?

Cost Explorer, grouped by service and usage type. Common culprits are data transfer out to the internet, cross-AZ traffic charged both ways, [NAT gateways](card:internet-and-nat-gateways) and resources left running idle. Then I set an AWS Budgets alert so the next jump is caught early.
