## What it is

A Service is a Kubernetes object that gives a group of Pods one stable address. It picks its Pods with a [label selector](card:namespaces-labels-selectors), for example `app: payment-service`. Kubernetes gives the Service a virtual IP, called the ClusterIP, and a DNS name. Callers use that address, and the Service forwards each connection to one of the matching Pods.

## Why it exists

A [Deployment](card:deployments-and-replicasets) replaces Pods all the time: during a rollout, after a crash, or when a node fails. Each new Pod gets a new IP. If order-service stored the IP of a payment Pod, its calls would break at the next rollout. It would also have to choose between three replicas by itself. A Service removes both problems: the address never changes, and Kubernetes keeps the list of Pods behind it up to date.

## How it works

1. You create the Service with a selector and ports. Kubernetes gives it a ClusterIP and a DNS name.
2. A controller in the control plane finds the Pods whose labels match the selector, in the same namespace. It writes their IPs, ports and ready state into EndpointSlice objects.
3. [kube-proxy](card:kubernetes-architecture) on every node reads the Services and EndpointSlices and programs forwarding rules on that node.
4. order-service opens a connection to `payment-service:80`. The rules on its node pick one ready Pod, at random by default, and send the connection to that Pod's port 8080.
5. When a Pod is replaced, the EndpointSlice changes and the rules follow. The ClusterIP stays the same.

## Example

The Service for the payment service's three Pods in the `payments` namespace:

```yaml
apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: payment-service
  namespace: payments
spec:
  type: ClusterIP          # the default; may be left out
  selector:
    app: payment-service   # must match the Pod template labels
  ports:
    - port: 80             # callers use payment-service:80
      targetPort: 8080     # Spring Boot listens here
```

What to notice:

- The selector is the only link between the Service and the Pods. If the Deployment's Pod labels say `app: payments`, the Service matches nothing and every call fails, though the Pods are healthy.
- `port: 80` and `targetPort: 8080` differ on purpose. Callers write `http://payment-service`, and the app keeps its own port.
- Remove the `type` line and nothing changes, because ClusterIP is the default. The Service is then reachable only from inside the cluster; outside traffic comes through [Ingress or Gateway API](card:ingress-and-gateway-api).

## Rules and defaults

- **Default type:** ClusterIP (Kubernetes 1.37).
- **targetPort:** defaults to the same value as `port`. It can also be the name of a container port, such as `http`, so the number can change in one place.
- **Readiness:** EndpointSlices list each Pod with a ready flag. Only ready Pods get new connections; a Pod counts as ready when its readiness check passes, which you will see in [probes](card:probes).
- **No ready Pods:** the Service still exists, but calls to it fail.
- **EndpointSlices:** Kubernetes starts a new slice once the existing ones hold 100 endpoints each. The older Endpoints API is deprecated since Kubernetes 1.33.
- **Session affinity:** `None` by default. `sessionAffinity: ClientIP` sends one client IP to the same Pod, for 10800 seconds (3 hours) by default.
- **Headless Service:** `clusterIP: None` gives no virtual IP; DNS then returns the Pod IPs, as [Pod networking and DNS](card:cluster-networking-dns) shows.

## Not to be confused with

A Deployment keeps the right number of Pods running and replaces them when they fail. A Service gives those Pods one address and spreads connections over them. Rule: the Deployment decides how many Pods run; the Service decides how callers reach them.

## Common mistakes

- **"The Service balances every HTTP request."** It works per connection. A client that reuses one long-lived connection, as HTTP/2 and gRPC clients do, keeps talking to one Pod.
- **Calling a Pod IP directly.** It works until the Pod is replaced. Call the Service name instead.

## Interview Qs

### A Service exists, but calls to it fail. What do you check first?

Its endpoints: `kubectl get endpointslices -n payments -l kubernetes.io/service-name=payment-service`. No addresses means the selector matches no Pod labels. Addresses that are not ready point to a readiness problem. Then check that targetPort is the port the app listens on. You will see the full order in [debugging with kubectl](card:kubectl-debugging).

### What is the difference between port, targetPort and nodePort?

port is the Service's own port, which callers dial (80). targetPort is the container port the traffic is sent to (8080). nodePort is a port opened on every node, from 30000-32767 by default, and exists only for NodePort and LoadBalancer Services.

### Why not let clients call the Pod IPs directly?

Pod IPs change whenever a Pod is replaced, and clients would have to track every replica and its health themselves. A Service gives one fixed address and keeps the list of ready Pods current, so clients need no changes during rollouts or failures.
