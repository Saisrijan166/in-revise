## Why it exists

Paying needs five classes called in a fixed order. If every controller does that itself, the order is repeated everywhere and any change breaks many callers.

## How it works

<Mermaid
  alt="A controller calls pay on PaymentFacade. The facade calls, in order, Validator, FraudCheck, PaymentGateway, Receipts and Notifier."
  chart={`flowchart LR
  C[Controller] -- "pay(request)" --> F[PaymentFacade]
  F --> V[Validator]
  F --> R[FraudCheck]
  F --> G[PaymentGateway]
  F --> E[Receipts]
  F --> N[Notifier]`}
/>

- The facade only orchestrates; real work stays in the subsystem classes.
- If it grows too big, split it into smaller facades before it becomes a [god object](card:anti-patterns).
- Facade vs Mediator is covered under [Mediator](card:mediator).

## Example

```java
// Without a facade, every caller repeats the steps, in the right order:
validator.check(request);
fraud.screen(request);
gateway.charge(request.amount());
Receipt receipt = receipts.issue(request);
notifier.paid(receipt);

// With a facade, callers make one call:
Receipt same = paymentFacade.pay(request);
```

## Interview Qs

### What problem does Facade solve?

It shields callers from a complex subsystem. They depend on one simple class, so a change inside the subsystem is made once, in the facade, not in every caller.

### Give a real example of a facade in Java.

SLF4J, the Simple Logging Facade for Java. Code logs through its API, and the actual framework, such as Logback or java.util.logging, is chosen at deployment.

### Facade or Adapter?

An adapter makes one existing interface usable by converting it. A facade defines a new, simpler interface over a whole subsystem of objects.
