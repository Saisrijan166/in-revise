## What it is

A chunk is a small passage of a document, such as a paragraph or a section, that is embedded and stored as one unit in the vector store. Chunking is the step in [the RAG pipeline](card:rag-pipeline) that cuts each document into these passages before they are embedded. Retrieval returns whole chunks, so a chunk is the smallest thing a search can hand to the model. How you cut the document decides what the model gets to read.

## Why it exists

You cannot embed a whole 100-page PDF as one vector. The [embedding](card:embeddings) model has an input token limit, and a single vector for 100 pages matches almost every question weakly, because it averages away the detail. If you then stuffed the whole PDF into the prompt, you would pay for thousands of tokens and bury the one relevant line in noise. Chunking fixes both: small passages fit the embedding model, and search can return the exact paragraph about UPI refunds instead of the entire document.

## How it works

You choose a splitting strategy to suit the documents.

| Strategy | How it splits | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed size | every N tokens, with overlap | plain text, a quick start |
| Sentence-aware | fixed size, but cut at `.`, `?`, `!` or a newline | most prose |
| By structure | at headings, sections or pages | policies, FAQs, Markdown, HTML |
| Semantic | where the topic changes | long text that mixes topics |

1. Pick the strategy, then split each document into chunks of a few hundred tokens.
2. Add overlap: start each chunk with the last sentence or two of the previous one, so a fact split across a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk.
3. Give each chunk context: prepend its document title or section, so a short chunk still carries its subject.
4. Embed and store each chunk, then measure retrieval on real questions and adjust the size and overlap.

So the key is that the cut must keep each chunk small but still self-contained.

## Example

Here two consecutive chunks overlap by one sentence, so the UPI line is complete in both.

```text
Chunk 1: ... Refunds go back to the original payment method.
         UPI refunds reach the customer in 5-7 working days.
Chunk 2: UPI refunds reach the customer in 5-7 working days.   <- overlap
         Card refunds can take up to 10 working days. ...
(fictional example policy)
```

What to notice:

- The UPI sentence sits on the boundary between the two chunks, so without overlap it could be cut in half and retrieved with half its meaning.
- Repeating it at the start of Chunk 2 means a search for "UPI refund" matches a whole, meaningful passage either way.
- The cost is real: overlap creates more chunks, so more storage, more embedding calls, and near-duplicate hits in results that you may need to deduplicate.
- Removing the overlap would shrink the index but risk splitting the answer across two chunks, so neither ranks well.

## Rules and defaults

- **Spring AI 2.0 `TokenTextSplitter`:** chunks at 800 tokens, cuts at sentence boundaries (`.`, `?`, `!`, newline), and does not support overlap; add overlap with custom logic if you need it.
- **OpenAI file search:** defaults to 800-token chunks with 400-token overlap, and lets you set a chunk size between 100 and 4,096 tokens, capping overlap at half the chunk size.
- **No universal size:** the best size depends on your documents and questions, so treat any default as a starting point and confirm it with evals.
- **Keep chunks self-contained:** always store the title or section with the chunk, or use [contextual retrieval](card:contextual-chunk-context), so a retrieved chunk makes sense alone.

## Interview Qs

### How do you choose chunk size?

Start from a documented default such as a few hundred tokens, look at the shape of the documents, then measure retrieval recall on real questions and adjust. Smaller chunks suit precise, factual lookups; larger chunks keep more surrounding context for broader questions.

### Why use overlap, and what does it cost?

Overlap keeps an answer that falls on a chunk boundary whole in at least one chunk, so it still ranks and gets retrieved. It costs more: more chunks to store and embed, higher ingestion cost, and near-duplicate results you may have to deduplicate before building the prompt.

### Why does one giant chunk hurt retrieval?

A large chunk covers many topics, so its single embedding matches many questions weakly and none strongly, and it fills the prompt with irrelevant text. Precise, focused chunks produce sharper vectors that match the specific question and keep the prompt clean.
