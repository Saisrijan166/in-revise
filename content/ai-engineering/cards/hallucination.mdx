## What it is

A hallucination is an answer from an [LLM](card:what-is-an-llm) that is stated fluently and confidently but is false, or is not supported by the information you supplied. NIST calls the same thing "confabulation". It is not a rare bug; it follows directly from how the model works, since it generates likely-sounding text rather than retrieving checked facts. The danger is that a hallucinated answer looks exactly like a correct one.

## Why it exists

The model writes the most likely continuation, not a verified fact, so when it lacks the real information it fills the gap with something plausible. Asked about a refund policy it never saw, it may answer "refunds arrive in 2 days" and sound certain. On top of that, both training and common benchmarks reward a confident guess more than an honest "I don't know" (Kalai et al., 2025), so the model learns to guess rather than abstain.

## How it works

There is no single switch to turn hallucination off; you reduce it with a stack of mitigations, each owned by a later concept.

1. Ground the answer in retrieved sources so the model has the real facts in front of it ([RAG](card:what-is-rag)).
2. Ask the model to answer only from those sources and to say it does not know when they do not cover the question.
3. Constrain and check the output: [citations](card:grounding-and-citations), [structured output](card:structured-output), then [validation in code](card:output-validation).
4. Measure the result with [evaluation](card:ai-evaluation), and route high-risk answers to a human.

So the key is that each layer catches what the one before misses; none of them alone is enough.

## Example

The cheapest single mitigation is explicit: tell the model it may abstain.

```text
Weak prompt:  "What is the refund window for order 42?"
              -> may invent a number if the policy is not supplied

Grounded prompt:
  "Use only the policy text below. If it does not answer the question,
   reply exactly: I don't have that information.
   Policy: <retrieved chunks>
   Question: What is the refund window for order 42?"
```

What to notice:

- The grounded prompt supplies the real policy, so the model has facts instead of a gap to fill.
- Telling it to reply "I don't have that information" gives it a safe way out, so a confident wrong guess is less likely (Anthropic docs).
- This is a prompt, not a guarantee: the model can still ignore the instruction, which is why validation and evaluation sit on top.

## Interview Qs

### How do you reduce hallucinations?

Ground the model in retrieved sources, improve retrieval quality, and explicitly let it say "I don't know". Then require citations, constrain and validate the output in code, evaluate on test cases, and add human review for high-risk answers. No single step is enough; they stack.

### Why do LLMs hallucinate at all?

They predict plausible text rather than looking facts up, so a gap in knowledge gets filled with something that sounds right. Training and benchmarks also reward a confident guess over admitting uncertainty, and missing context or stale knowledge makes guessing more likely.
